Understanding ADHD

What is ADHD?

ADHD stands for Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder. It's a neurodevelopmental condition that affects how the brain regulates attention, impulses, and activity levels. ADHD is not laziness, lack of intelligence, or a behavioral problem—it's a difference in brain chemistry and structure.

How Common is ADHD?

About 5-7% of children and 2-5% of adults have ADHD. Many people aren't diagnosed until later in life. You're not alone.

The Three Types of ADHD

1. Inattentive Type

Difficulty focusing, organizing, and completing tasks. People with this type may:

- Struggle to pay attention in school or work

- Lose things frequently

- Have trouble organizing tasks

- Daydream or seem "spaced out"

2. Hyperactive-Impulsive Type

Excessive restlessness and impulsivity. People with this type may:

- Feel constantly fidgety or restless

- Talk excessively

- Interrupt others frequently

- Act without thinking about consequences

3. Combined Type

A mix of both inattentive and hyperactive-impulsive symptoms. This is the most common type.

What Happens in the ADHD Brain?

The ADHD brain has differences in how it produces neurotransmitters—chemicals that help brain cells communicate. Specifically:

- Lower levels of dopamine (affects motivation and focus)

- Lower levels of norepinephrine (affects attention and alertness)

- Different brain structure in areas that control attention and impulse control

This isn't something you caused. It's how your brain is wired.

Myths vs. Facts

Myth: ADHD is just an excuse for bad behavior.

Fact: ADHD is a medical condition with biological and genetic roots.

Myth: Only hyperactive kids have ADHD.

Fact: Many people have inattentive ADHD and go undiagnosed for years.

Myth: You'll outgrow ADHD.

Fact: ADHD continues into adulthood for most people, though it may look different.

Why Diagnosis Matters

Getting diagnosed means:

- Understanding why you struggle with certain things

- Access to treatment (medication, therapy, strategies)

- School/work accommodations

- Reduced shame and self-blame
